GeoSense Field Manual

Every model, every measurement, every number — in plain language, with the honest failures included. Written so you can answer any question about how it works and why it decides what it decides.

1 · What the system actually does

You draw a box on a map and type what you are looking for. The system searches the live satellite archive, downloads only the pixels inside your box, watches how that ground behaved over two years, and tells you where something genuinely changed — with a size in hectares, a date, and a statistical guarantee about how often it is wrong.

The single most important idea: most "change" in satellite imagery is not change. Crops green up and die back. The sun moves. Haze comes and goes. A naive system reports all of it. Nearly everything in GeoSense exists to remove those false alarms and leave the real events.

scale

Where the numbers come from

An area roughly 9 km across is read into a 256×256 pixel picture, then grouped into a 16×16 grid of 256 cells, each about 550 m wide. Every cell gets its own two-year history. All decisions happen per cell.

indexed

What has been run so far

31 runs · 148 detections · 350 real satellite scenes · 51 stored image embeddings. Nothing is pre-downloaded; every run queries the live archive.

The one clever idea in how we use RemoteCLIP

The obvious thing is to ask the model "does this look like construction?" That fails badly, because half of any Indian scene already contains buildings — you would just rank existing cities. So we embed each place twice, before and after, and score the difference:

score = similarity(after, your words) − similarity(before, your words)

Only places that moved toward your description score highly. We are searching for change in meaning, not for appearance.

Why we embed only ~4 places, not all 256. RemoteCLIP takes 858 ms per image on this laptop's CPU. All 256 cells × 2 images each would be about seven minutes. So the cheap statistics run first and propose a shortlist, and only that shortlist is shown to the expensive model. This is the same "tip-and-cue" logic the architecture applies to satellites — applied to compute instead.

4 · What we actually measure

Satellites record brightness in several colours, including two infrared bands your eye cannot see. Combining them gives numbers that mean something physical. All four run from −1 to +1.

IndexFormulaIn plain wordsTypical values
NDVI(NIR−Red)/(NIR+Red) How much living plant matter. Healthy leaves reflect near-infrared strongly. forest 0.8 · crops 0.4 · desert 0.1
NDBI(SWIR−NIR)/(SWIR+NIR) How built-up or hard-surfaced. Concrete and roofs are bright in shortwave infrared. city +0.2 · field −0.2
MNDWI(Green−SWIR)/(Green+SWIR) How wet. Water absorbs shortwave infrared almost completely. water > 0 · dry land −0.7
BSI(SWIR+Red)−(NIR+Blue)
over their sum		 How bare the soil is — no plants, no water, no buildings. bare +0.2 · vegetated −0.2

How your words become measurements

MiniLM matches your sentence to one of nine change types. Each type carries the physical directions its indices move — that part is physics, not guesswork: removing vegetation does lower NDVI.

You ask forSystem expects
new constructionNDVI ↓ 1.0 · NDBI ↑ 1.0 · BSI ↑ 0.6
deforestationNDVI ↓ 1.2 · BSI ↑ 0.5  (requires forest before)

floodingMNDWI ↑ 1.2 · NDVI ↓ 0.5

Those weights are combined into a single number per cell per date, pointing in the direction of the thing you asked about. That combined number is what everything downstream tests. Run summary → Composite

5 · How we avoid reporting nonsense

This is the part that makes the system trustworthy, and the part worth explaining slowly to a judge. Four defences, applied in order.

Defence 1 — Subtract the seasons

Crops go green and brown every year. If you compare March to October you will always find "change". So we fit a smooth yearly wave to each cell's history and subtract it. What remains is only what the calendar cannot explain.

A measured detail worth mentioning: we deliberately do not include a straight-line trend term. Over a two-year window it cannot be estimated reliably and it extrapolates catastrophically — with it, pure noise scored as strongly as real change in our tests. Removing it fixed that.

Defence 2 — Find the step, don't assume the date

We try every possible split date and keep the one where before and after differ most. That is how the system reports when a change started instead of being told. Evidence → break date

Defence 3 — Ask what noise can fake

This is the core trick. We shuffle the dates into a random order — which destroys any real event but keeps the noise exactly as it was — and re-run the whole search. Repeat 140 times over 160 cells and you get about 22,400 examples of the biggest "change" pure noise can invent in this scene, at this cloudiness, with this many dates.

A real detection has to beat that. So the threshold isn't a number we chose — the data sets it. Run summary → Null draws

Defence 4 — Correct for testing 256 places at once

Testing 256 cells means even a strict 1-in-100 threshold gives you two or three false alarms every run. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure raises the bar to compensate, and lets us make a promise with real content:

"At α = 0.10, at most 10% of the cells we certify are expected to be false." Not a confidence score somebody invented — a controlled error rate. When we measured it, the true rate was 0% against a nominal 10%: the guarantee holds, conservatively.

This is why runs often return zero certified detections. That is the system being honest, not broken. Everything below the bar is still listed as a candidate with its p-value.

6 · Accuracy — and how we measured it

Public change-detection benchmarks (OSCD, LEVIR-CD) give you two dates and a mask. Our detector needs 15+ dates to fit a seasonal model, so scoring against them would measure a different algorithm. Instead we do what a radar engineer does to test a receiver: take real signal, inject a known target, and see how small a target we can still find.

We take a genuine 23-date Sentinel-2 stack over Gurugram — real noise, real seasons, real cloud gaps — inject a change of known size at a known place, and measure how often the detector finds it.

8 · A real failure, explained

Lead with this if you get a hostile question. Being able to explain your own failure precisely is worth more than pretending you have none.

What happened: we asked for flooding over a desert area near Pali, Rajasthan. The system returned four detections and certified one. There was no flood.

certified cell[15,14]
  ΔMNDWI  +0.083   (wetness rose slightly)
  ΔNDVI   −0.155   (vegetation fell)
  ΔNDBI   +0.055   (hard/bare surface ROSE)
  ΔBSI    +0.046   (bare soil ROSE)

Why that cannot be a flood: water absorbs shortwave infrared almost completely, so under real water NDBI and BSI fall. Here they rose. This is dry ground getting drier and barer — the opposite.

Three separate causes, all worth naming:

  1. We test the direction of change, never the resulting state. MNDWI going from −0.80 to −0.72 scores the same as −0.1 to +0.4. Real water needs MNDWI above roughly 0. There is no absolute gate.
  2. The flood recipe only looks at two of four indices. It uses MNDWI and NDVI and ignores NDBI and BSI — the very two that would have vetoed every one of these detections.
  3. The models classify the question, not the answer. MiniLM read your words; RemoteCLIP re-ordered the shortlist. No stage ever asked "is this actually a flood?"

The fix is change-type classification: classify each detection from its own evidence rather than only testing what was asked, add absolute gates (a flood must end up wet), and warn when the evidence disagrees with the query. That work is identified and not yet built.

9 · Question bank

How is the query classified, and on what parameters?
Your sentence is converted to a 384-number vector by all-MiniLM-L6-v2. Each of nine change types has a written description, also converted to a vector. We take the closest match by cosine similarity, and report the gap to the runner-up as a confidence margin. No keywords are matched. If the margin is small, the interface says LOW CONFIDENCE instead of pretending.
Why not just use keywords?
We did, and it failed silently. "landslide and built up damage" matched the letters "built" and searched for new construction — the opposite of what was asked — without ever saying so. Keyword rules scored 7/11 on our test queries; the model scores 10/11 and flags its own uncertainty.
Did you train these models?
No. Both are pretrained and used zero-shot. We have no labelled training data. This is a feature, not an excuse: nothing has been fitted to our own test cases, so the numbers are not self-flattering.
What is your accuracy?
Minimum detectable change ΔNDVI ≈ 0.08 at 80% detection, 100% precision from 0.06 upward, and 0 false alarms measured on unchanged imagery against a promised ceiling of 10%. Most teams cannot state a detection limit at all.
Why does it sometimes find nothing?
Because it is testing 256 places at once and correcting for that. With ~15 usable dates, only large clean changes clear the bar. Zero certified detections is a statement about the data, not a crash — and everything below the bar is still listed with its p-value.
Confidence says 99.6% but it's marked "below α". Why?
Fair catch, and the wording is poor. "Confidence" is 1 − p for that one cell in isolation. "Certified" additionally requires surviving the correction for testing 256 cells simultaneously. A cell can be individually unusual yet not survive that correction. The two numbers answer different questions.
Why DuckDB and not PostgreSQL with pgvector?
This machine has no PostgreSQL, no Docker and no admin rights. Both sit behind the same interface, so swapping is a connection string. Be precise: pgvector is a PostgreSQL extension and does not run in DuckDB, so we do not claim it. What does run is native in-database cosine search over a fixed-width 512-number column, verified identical to a NumPy calculation.
Why not SAM for segmentation?
SAM segments objects by appearance. We need the extent of a change, which is already defined by the statistic that raised the alarm. Deriving the footprint from that same statistic means the reported hectares can never contradict the detection — and it needs no model, no GPU and no download.
Can it run offline, as the rules require?
Not yet, and this is the most serious gap. Every scene is fetched live from public archives at query time. With networking disabled the system does nothing. Local staging is designed but unbuilt.
Can you find "large vehicle concentrations"?
No — and the system now says so. Sentinel-2 pixels are 10 m across; a truck is 2 m. That query returns LOW CONFIDENCE with a margin of 0.021. Detecting vehicles needs sub-metre commercial imagery.

Say the gaps out loud before you are asked. A team that can name what its system cannot do, precisely and without flinching, reads as more competent than one claiming everything works — especially to judges who will find the gaps anyway.
